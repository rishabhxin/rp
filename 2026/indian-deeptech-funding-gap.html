India's Deeptech Problem Isn't Talent. It's Risk Capital.

@rishabhxin|November 21, 2026·3 min read

India wants to become a global technology powerhouse, but there is a gap between our ambition and where private investment is actually going.

Deeptech startups working in semiconductors, robotics, advanced materials, biotechnology, space technology and artificial intelligence face a fundamentally different funding environment from conventional software or consumer startups. They require longer R&D cycles, specialised infrastructure, expensive testing and significantly more patient capital.

The problem isn't that these businesses lack commercial potential. It's that their path to commercialisation is harder to predict. Venture investors often look for faster validation, shorter paths to revenue and clearer exit opportunities. Deeptech rarely offers that comfort.

The numbers reflect this challenge. According to Inc42's 2025 funding analysis, deeptech received just $1.6 billion out of $164 billion in cumulative Indian startup funding between 2014 and 2025, less than 1%. In its survey of institutional investors, 36% identified the lack of patient venture capital (10+ years) as the biggest barrier to deeptech innovation.

There are signs of progress. The Nasscom–Zinnov report recorded $2.3 billion in Indian deeptech funding in 2025, a 37% increase year-on-year. But AI accounted for 91% of that funding, suggesting that capital is still concentrated in particular technology categories.

India also needs to address a broader research funding problem. NITI Aayog has highlighted that India's gross expenditure on R&D (GERD) stands at approximately 0.65% of GDP and that the shortage of patient capital makes it difficult to commercialise research.

This is not an argument against investing in consumer businesses or profitable startups. It is an argument for building a more balanced investment ecosystem.

We cannot aspire to compete with countries that lead in semiconductors, advanced manufacturing, robotics and biotechnology while expecting every startup to demonstrate quick revenue growth.

India doesn't just need more startups. It needs more companies building technology that the rest of the world will eventually depend on.

The question is whether our investment ecosystem is willing to fund that journey, including the years when the returns are not yet visible.
